\section{Introduction}

Humans rely heavily on the exchange of emotional information, especially when
personal aspects such as trust, intention and appraisals are to be communicated.
Most emotional cues that human would instinctively use for that purpose, do not translate well into
digital communication channels. It has however been found that the use pictorial facial expressions in the
shape of emoticons or expressive emojis did fill a gap
that written words can't match. Research has demonstrated that affective information
is transferred more effectively when appropriate non-verbal icons, such as facial expressions, are used
\cite{BOUTET2021106722,EmojisAsSocialInformation}. In particular, inter-annotator agreement on
the sentiment expressed by short messages was higher when they contained emojis\cite{SentimentOfEmoji}.
It seems logical that computers would use similar methods to display either their own emotions or
those they detect or predict. Yet, for some reason, this is not the case. Currently, no known
application exists where humans and computers engage in meaningful interaction through the use
of emojis or other emotional expressions. Despite significant advancements in human language generation,
there has been little progress in incorporating emotional cues. To make matters worse, the inability to
display appropriate emotional indicators hampers the field of robotics, as the lack of empathy makes
robots appear less intelligent and less trustworthy\cite{EmotionTowardsRobots, CHUAH2021102551, uncannyvalley}.
Visualising an appropriate emotion with an appropriate facial expression seems to be a very difficult
task for an algorithm.

Since humans intuitively use emoticons to encode and decode emotional information, a natural
starting point would be to extract the meaning of these icons first. Three methods have been employed
for this emotional translation: extracting meaning from context, gathering explicit human annotations,
or analyzing the facial expressions anatomically. Unfortunately, none of those methods yielded satisfactory
results. Classifying emojis by context gives precise numerical embeddings, revealing similarity clusters
within the emoji set\cite{WhatDoesThisEmojiMean}, but do not translate into human experience.
Direct questioning of humans reveals significant differences across cultures,
even when assessing just the single dimension on the spectrum from positive to negative\cite{emoji_intensity}.
The largest disagreement was repeatedly found for the ``grinning face''.
Its vastly different cultural connotations are further complicated by a count of 17 distinct graphical variations,
depending on the software brand and version displaying it\cite{emoji_interpretation}.
A widely used method to gain an objective assessment of the embedded emotional states is the Facial
Action Coding System (FACS), a common framework for analyzing facial expressions.
The method has been applied to emojis, but due to the lack of precision in the Unicode Consortium's
standardization efforts and the wide variability between software platforms this did not lead to
a consistent translation into emotional experiences\cite{emoji_facs}.
The bottom line is that emojis, albeit standardized coarsely by appearance, are
not suitable to objectively encode nuanced and precise emotions.

There is further evidence that facial emoticons outperform words and numbers for the communication of emotional experiences.
They can offer fine-grained scales for the description of qualias, such as pain and taste.
Research has shown them to be more objective and reliable than alternative methods.
Two such scales of gradually changing faces, from happy to painful, were designed to capture the level of perceived pain
in children\cite{bakerwong} and adult veterans\cite{dvprs}. It was found that these visual scales of facial expressions
allowed patients to communicate their personal experience with higher accuracy.
More stable and consistent results in self-reported pain greatly improved treatment.
Another application for face-visualized measurement scales was used in surveys for agreement to various questions.
Different designs of smiley faces were tested and shown to impact engagement in children\cite{likertscale}.
A wider range of emotional experiences can be expressed if additional dimensions are allowed.
An emoji grid spans two dimensions, valence and arousal, with selected emojis along the axes\cite{emojigrid}.
It was found useful for reporting food elicited emotions.
In short, these custom sets are consistently defined and outperform words on their specific domain.
However, their expressive range is limited and does not nearly cover the variety of Unicode faces.

For a greater range of facial expressions there is active research into facial visualisation techniques.
This includes 2D graphics\cite{parametric_face_facs, FACSGen} as well as physical 3D models\cite{FARAJ2021e00117}.
These techniques seek for realism in the displayed faces. By adding real world details they loose focus on the emotion itself.
They mimic certain facial expressions, but don't limit them to what is necessary to convey the emotion.
Another --almost ancient-- technique for facial visualisation uses very reduced line graphics
driven by high dimensional data\cite{chernoff}. Chernoff faces provide unique facial features controlled by 18 parameters covering the
sizes of eyes, mouth and ears as well as angles and distances between them. It was claimed, but later dismissed
that these faces can be processed instinctively without focusing on the geometric parameters attentively\cite{chernoff_analysis}.
Indeed, the multiple parameters do not include any of the subtle activations that are so characteristic of deep emotions.
Nevertheless, Chernoff faces come closest at being a predecessor, or next best solution, to the one I present in this paper.
Both solutions use line art and an exaggerated comic style to visualise data. However, with regards to intuitive
apprehension I want to argue that my visualization is able to transport both, quantitative and emotional information.


\section{What are emotions}

Emotions are transitory phenomenological experiences, showing multiple physiological adaptations,
formed by evolution to meet specific challenges to our survival\cite{Russell2009,Scarantino2012}.
The physiological changes include heart beat, respiration, posture, voice modulation, immune reactions and facial expressions.
Computers don't have these conscious experiences, and it seems that the best we can do is to capture
the visible changes, such as shown in face and voice. However, computers do inhabit the same physical world as we do and,
as such, share some of the selection pressures to which analogue adaptations exist. Hence, two important use
cases for emotional visualization exist: the display of an emotion detected inside a conscious being, and
the indication of a situation for which corresponding adaptations proved useful.

The idea of expressing a human emotion in quantitative terms was first raised by Willhelm Wundt in 1896.
He suggested a compositional model based on the dimensions of pleasantness, excitement and tension\cite{wundt,tridimensional}.
In 1957 Osgood performed a factor analysis on annotated associations between nouns and adjectives\cite{potency_osgood}.
He found three numerical factors that make up most of the meaning that human participants attributed to a generic list of words.
The factors were pleasantness (good-bad), potency (strong-weak) and activity (fast-slow). In 1980 Mehrabian applied this
analysis to a vocabulary more specific to emotions and derived the naming scheme of pleasure, arousal and dominance, also
known as the PAD model\cite{dominance_mehrabian}. These dimensions can be easily described and understood by humans,
allowing us to annotate subjective feelings. One could argue that this is just an arbitrary characterization of some
aspects of a human experiences, however, these dimensions have a clear functional role in our evolutionary history.
Moreover, while not complete, they can be used to classify a wide range of cognitive and physiological response programs.

Each of these three dimensions has distinct cognitive and physiological impacts, influencing specific desires and behaviors.
Pleasure (often called valence) represents an evaluation of the organism's state, driving short-term decisions and motivation.
The anticipated level of pleasure affects the selection among different options \cite{CABANAC1992173}.
Arousal indicates the probability and expected intensity of a response, guiding energy distribution, attention, and memory
formation \cite{storbeck2008}.
Dominance reflects perceived or claimed power within social hierarchies. It is triggered by competition outcomes, such as winning
or losing, and is most evident in emotions like shame and pride when defending or asserting a social rank \cite{weisfeld2012}.

There is an ongoing debate on how many more dimensions are needed to capture all emotions, or whether emotions should be
described in terms of dimensions at all. The theory of basic emotions argues for the existence of distinct categories
of emotional states, such as joy, anger, fear, sadness, surprise, disgust\cite{Ekman72,ortony1990}. However, there
is clear evidence that not all emotions fall into mutually exclusive categories. There are multiple ways in which they
can blend and mix gradually in various degrees. Multiple suggestions have been made to resolve the question for the precise
topology of the domain\cite{Scherer2007,study5,ZHOU202038}, but current technological capabilities are insufficient to
settle this debate. The much bigger problem might be that human language uses inherently categorical terms,
while computers are bound to work with dimensional decompositions, also known as embeddings. Developing a method to
bridge these two approaches would be valuable, even before the actual structure of our brain is fully mapped out.


\section{The parametric emoji}

We humans are able to communicate our emotions efficiently through facial expressions.
We rely on this channel in daily interactions as much as in our electronic communications.
There has been an intense debate on how far the specific expressions are
socially learned or genetically determined. Paul Ekman found large communality across cultures regarding
the response to emotional stimuli, concluding that the predetermined component is strong\cite{Ekman1971, Ekman72}.
However, cultural display rules can significantly alter the show of emotions based on social norms and contexts\cite{elfenbein2002}.
It would be a mistake to assume that the human face shows an internal state all the time.
Real emotions may be hard to catch as they may show only as microexpression an subsecond timescales.
Nevertheless, there are widely shared rules for the decoding of facial expressions that we
actively use to promote our emotions and that we share among ourselves in theatre plays, games, cartoons and movies.

In this article I want to propose the Paramoji as a facial icon, similar to the faces standardized in the set of emoji.
Instead of enumerating the icons sequentially, the proposed graphics is parametric. It can be smoothly
combined from five individual dimensions. The mapping from emotion to expression follows the assumption
that facial features represent distinct components of our emotional state\cite{Componential}.
The resulting images are comically exaggerated to simplify their decoding. Their appearance is designed to align with
representations in prevalent cultures and thus understandable to a wide audience. To limit their
application to emotional states the icon is reduced to a minimum of features, eliminating any
references to demographic properties like age, gender and race.

The graphical features of the Paramoji depend linearly on the defining emotional dimensions.
This makes it possible to treat the icon as a gauge that transforms the raw parameters linearly into coordinates.
Obviously, it would have been preferable to construct a scale that is linear in the expressed emotional intensity.
However, we would hardly be able to agree on a method for its measurement.
Linearity in graphics is more than just the second best option.
It can be checked objectively, as the generating code essentially consists of a matrix
vector multiplication and subsequent plotting commands.
Finally, linearity creates the benefit that any detected smooth
transition from one state to another would also show smoothly and without artefacts when animated. A midpoint
in parameter space is guaranteed to also be the midpoint in its graphical representation and vice versa.


\subsection{Valence and arousal}

The circumplex model is the simplest numerical model for emotions\cite{circumplex}.
It defines two dimensions of valence and arousal.
Valance, previously called pleasure, defines the axis from pleasurable and positive to miserable and negative.
The second dimension is arousal and describes the axis from aroused, agitated and active to calm, sleepy and inactive.
Neurological evidence provides some indication that these two dimensions actually exist and correlate to
measurable brain activities\cite{circumplex2}.

Figure \ref{fig:circumplex} shows the chosen visualisation for these first two dimensions.
The graphics is defined through the control points of Bezier lines with some rotation of the eyes' outlines.
All coordinates and angles are mapped linearly from the two input dimensions.
In this (still incomplete) model arousal controls eye and mouth sizes and valence controls curvature.

Arousal directly maps to the area covered by the eyes and mouth.
High values of arousal align with the emotion of surprise, raising inner and outer eye brows (AU1+AU2), raising the upper lip (AU5)
and letting the jaw drop (AU26).
There is a minimal amount of visible activation at 0.0 when eyes and mouth stay totally shut.
On the other end of the spectrum there is no physical limit on how wide a comic eye can open and the choice for the 1.0 position is arbitrary.
There is no absolute center or neutral position.

Valence (or pleasure) is drawn as the curvature of the eyes and the mouth.
There is a geometric center at 0.5 when eyes and mouth are flat.
Both bend upwards in pleasure depicting a raised cheek (AU6) and lips pulled upwards (AU12),
in accordance to the action units defined by the facial action coding system (FACS).
Asian and Western cultures put a varying degree of focus to these two features
as reflected in early text based emoticons \texttt{\^{}\_\^} and \texttt{:)}, with one only showing eyes and the other only the mouth.
The Paramoji yokes these features to avoid confusion.
For low values of valence eyes and mouth bend downwards. Linearity demands the expression to be graphically symmetric.
The resulting drooping upper eyelid corresponds to AU41, but also to the foreshortened view when the head is lowered.
The depressed lips (AU41) form the iconic sign of misery.

Looking at facial expressions one can find the predicted emotions on the perimeter of the domain.
Starting on the top in a clockwise direction we find: happy, excited, alarmed, distressed, miserable, depressed, sleepy and content.
More dimensions are needed to define more specific emotions.


\subsection{Dominance}

The dominance dimension differentiates the emotions on the axis from dominant to submissive.
It facilitates the formation of social hierarchies.
High values of dominance are associated with strong, hard, brave, tenacious, aggressive and winning.
Low dominance associates to weak, soft, cowardly, yielding, fearful and losing\cite{potency_osgood, dominance_mehrabian}.
The archetypical scenario under which people would show extreme values of dominance is after a competitive sportive
event that is either won or lost, when physical exhaustion leaves little energy for confounding facial displays.
An analysis of respective photographs revealed a number of significant features \cite{Composability}.
Most salient are the narrowed and lowered brows (AU4) in victorious conditions and the inner brow raise (AU1) upon defeat.
Corroborating evidence for this indicator is drawn from a study in which randomly generated faces were rated along
subjective assessments of trustworthiness and dominance \cite{FunctionalBasis}.
On the lower part of the face opposing indicator pairs are the relaxed mouth with a dropping jaw (AU26) and the
lip funneler (AU22) \cite{Composability}. The jaw position is understood to cause the show of teeth when combined
with parted lips. The lip funneler (AU22) is indicated with a sharper mouth contour. The raised cheeks (AU6) and
raised chin (AU17) is plotted by slightly lifting the lower part of the face.

In combination with low valence and high arousal the dominance dimension distinguishes between anger and fear.
It has been pointed out that anger mimics a strong and mature
individual, while fear has evolved to imitate a child or weak individual\cite{marsh2005}.
The Paramoji graphics seems to reflect the intended reason behind these expressions as they seek to communicate
ones claimed position in social hierarchies.


\subsection{Contempt}

Contempt arises from a sense of perceived moral superiority.
It is typically directed toward those considered incompetent or malicious.
This differs from dominance as it does not seek to initiate a correction within the existing social hierarchy.
Instead, it aims at avoidance or a ban\cite{fischer2007beat}.
Depending on one's ability to proceed with such a strategy, contempt can be a pleasurable or miserable experience.
When elicited towards inferior and shamed it can feel good, towards superiors it can feel like suppression.
Synonyms can be found in the adjectives scornful, haughty, smug, vain, or disdainful\cite{contempt_ekman}.
One finding also suggests that ``annoyance'' is an adequate label for this emotion\cite{contempt_new}.
The opposite of contempt would be admiration, a silent appreciation of some ones superiority, without
anticipation of interpersonal status changes.

The universally confirmed expression of contempt is AU14, the unilateral lip-tightener\cite{Ekman1986, contempt_ekman}.
Tension in the cheek can also press the eyes into squeezing, often show to deliberately amplify this state.
A squinting eye is not in itself a universal signal for contempt,
but a graphical choice to emphasize its asymmetric nature. A subtle side eye adds a trace of annoyance.
All effects are one-sided and limited to the left side of the face, an arbitrary choice,
because there is no known emotional meaning to chirality. There is no known facial feature indicating admiration.

The combinations of parameters produce sometimes comical, but geometrically valid facial expressions.


\subsection{Control}

The control dimension measures to what extent an inner experience is deliberately modulated
and any impulsive expression is suppressed. First introduced by Osgood in 1966, the
axis separates the adjective pairs intentional-unintentional, deliberate-impulsive,
controlled-uncontrolled \cite{control_osgood}. Despite scarce occurrence in psychological
literature, one study followed up on this terminology and
listed control as the first of 32 measured dimensions\cite{FRIJDA1969167}, defining it as the
``aspect of emotion which distinguishes stances
taken and initiated by the subject from those elicited by the environment.''

Although the concept of an intentional emotion might sound like an oxymoron at first,
there is a clear distinction between emotions that overwhelm the self and those that
leave rationality to plan out tactical details. The emotions of anger, fear and pain
serve their purpose by advertising their presence to society in an immediate urge to comply with
one's demands. The implicit threat of escalation works through a plausible loss of
rational self-control. The emotions of hatred, guilt and shame work differently.
Here, the demand for social adaption is delayed in order to conceal a temporary weakness (or insufficient strength).
Such emotions work on a slower time scale, nudging the self to look for a more suitable
opportunity to rectify the discovered inadequacy\cite{reed2014commitment,reed2014hatred,tracy2008shame}.

The control dimension in this article ranges from 0.0: uncontrolled, overwhelmed, expressed to
1.0: controlled, modulated, suppressed. The visualisation follows an hypothesis raised by Ekman and Friesen\cite{Ekman75}.
They wrote: ``when a person is controlling what is shown on his face, more effort is focused on managing what occurs
in and around his mouth and lips than in the area of the eyes/lids or brows/forehead.''
The major and easiest to control aspect of the displayed intensity is previously introduced as arousal ($a$).
The graphical impact can be separated into arousal shown in the eye region ($a_1$) and arousal
shown in the mouth region ($a_2$).
In a situation of maximum control, when proverbially biting one's tongue, the eyes are open in a strong display
of the inner state. Although closing one's mouth might be the most clumsily executed attempt at controlling
the display, it is a comical exaggeration that is not intended to mimic a real face, but instead aims
at understandability. In a situation of lost control, the opposite expression is demanded by symmetry.
The eyes are closed and the mouth is open. Nothing goes in. Everything comes out.

The amount of exerted control ($k$) can be computed as the difference between $a_1$ and $a_2$. For a controlled and
modulated expression $k$ is positive. For an uncontrolled, overwhelmed expression $k$ is negative, whereas $a_1$ and
$a_2$ can only be visually displayed for positive values, when mouth and eyes are at least as open as shut.
Equation \eqref{eqn:control} defines how the dimension can be converted.
The simplicity of the equations is commensurate with the psychological depth of this theoretical model.

\begin{equation}
  \label{eqn:control}
  \begin{array}{rclll}
      a_1 &=& a + k/2 &\mbox{for}& a > -k/2 \\
      a_2 &=& a - k/2 &\mbox{for}& a > k/2\\
      k &=& a_1 - a_2 \\
%      a &=& (a_1 + a_2)/2
  \end{array}
\end{equation}

Figure \ref{fig:control} shows the visual effect of the control dimension on some random faces.
It is interesting that even in the extremely overwhelmed versions one can still differentiate between high and low valence.
This is in contrast to real faces, where extreme pain and extreme joy cannot be distinguished from each other, unless
additional context or body language is given\cite{painjoy}.
The comical visualisations are not just exaggerated displays of real facial expressions. Their aim
is to show emotional information for easy decoding. It is not a guide for what real faces would look like.

\section{Empirical evidence}

The previous section outlined the graphical design choices for a parametric emoji that visualizes
emotions within a five-dimensional state. While every effort was made to ground these choices in rational
reasoning, it is important to acknowledge that many decisions were influenced by the author's subjective
judgment. To validate the credibility of the final outcome, empirical evidence is essential. This section
details the experiment conducted, including the recruitment of untrained participants for annotation tasks
and the process of extracting results from raw data. Specifically, I aim to demonstrate that the theoretical
derivation closely aligns with human-generated annotations.

\subsection{The experiment}

Figure \ref{fig:ui} shows the user interface that test participants had to navigate for the annotation of
emotional dimensions. Two faces were randomly drawn from a uniform distribution. The users' task was to order
them on the assigned emotional dimension. One had to be chosen as more intense and the other consistently as
less intense. Alternatively, both faces could be labeled as equal. When the two choices matched
participants could continue to the next pair. No additional explanation was given after following a link
to this emotion classification task.

 \begin{center}

 \end{center}
 \caption{

   one randomly selected emotional dimension.
 }

Each participant was assigned to one emotional dimension, to avoid any cross influence caused by switching contexts.
Each dimension was described by adjectives for their negative and positive extreme. Synonyms were given to avoid
miscommunication. The following list shows the five possible tasks descriptions to which participants were assigned.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Which face is more {\bf negative} (miserable, rejected); which is more {\bf positive} (pleasurable, accepted) ?
  \item Which face is more {\bf calm} (sleepy, relaxed, unimpressed); which is more {\bf aroused} (surprised, panicking, facing the unexpected) ?
  \item Which face is more {\bf submissive} (in doubt, passive, insecure); which is more {\bf dominant} (focused, strong, activated)?
  \item Which face is more {\bf uncontrolled} (overwhelmed, expressed); which is more {\bf controlled} (pretended, modulated, suppressed) ?
  \item Which face is more {\bf humble} (moderate, respectful); which is more {\bf contemptuous} (self-loving, vain, disdainful) ?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Participation}

Test participants were hired from MicroWorkers.com, a global market place for crowdsourced work.
A deep analysis of the workers' anatomy has been made previously and found to be still valid\cite{microworkers}.
The overall work force has a global bias towards India, Bangladesh and the Philippines with some participation
from the Western countries.

420 workers took part in this experiment and produced 13190 comparisons.
The pay was 1 Cent per entry, paid for batches of size 20 or 30. Some workers submitted two batches without extra reward.
The average time spent on one comparison was 10 seconds, the median 5 seconds.
It is likely that some workers have seen previous versions of the experiment, but never got any feedback on their performance.
The published data are the first results for a textual comparison task.

\subsection{Regression results}

The data were collected as five parameters for the Carthesian model and grouped by the
five dimensions that were rated. Additionally, a label was
stored to indicate whether it was seen as more, less or equally intense, yielding the rating numbers 1, 0 and 0.5.
Since all pairs were chosen fully at random
both sides of the comparison could be treated as independent samples. The average rating value of a
position in five-dimensional parameter space can be interpreted as the fraction of
Paramojis that it supersedes, i.e. its quantile on the measured emotional scale.

A linear regression of the reported rating numbers onto the Carthesian dimensions reveals
how much the report is swayed by each
theoretical dimension. Table table \ref{tab:raw_data} shows the results.
The 1-norm column evaluates the biggest possible change of the predicted quantile in two opposite corners (1,1,0,0,0) and (0,0,1,1,1).
In absence of doubt or disagreement the least intense position should yield 0 and the most intense should yield 1.
As we can see the reported change in valence can be explained through the graphical transformations\footnote{
The value for valence is greater than the valid probability range from 0 (always labeled less intense) to 1 (always labeled more intense).
This is because the parameters were estimated based on random points within the domain.
The corner points were not among the queried samples and thus the results here are extrapolated values}.
It is also interesting that just over half the effect on valence originates from the iconic curvatures controlled by $v$,
which many icon sets depend on solely. Pleasure is clearly more than a curved mouth.

The next thing to observe in table \ref{tab:raw_data} is that most rows are dominated by the $v$ column.
With exception of dominance
there is a high degree of collinearity between all rows. An aroused or contemptuous face was
likely also seen as negative. Control was
seen as positive. The describing adjectives were not understood as independent.

\subsection{Orthogonalization}

Apparently, respondents did not understand the meaning of the questions as orthogonal.
A better question to ask would have been along the line of “who looks more aroused,
without considering the effect of negativity?” However such questions would become quite
confusing when adding more dimensions.

The Gram-Schmidt method is a standard procedure to calculate such an orthogonalization mathematically.
Equation (\ref{eqn:gramschmidt}) shows the calculation. The vectors v are the linear dependent
input shown in the rows of table \ref{tab:raw_data}. The orthogonalized output u is seen in the rows
of table \ref{tab:ortho_data}.

\begin{equation}
  \mbox{u}_i
  \,=\,
  \mbox{v}_i - \sum\limits^{i-1}_{j=1}
    \frac{\langle
      \mbox{v}_i, \mbox{u}_j
    \rangle}
    {\langle
      \mbox{u}_j, \mbox{u}_j
    \rangle}
    \mbox{u}_j
  \label{eqn:gramschmidt}
\end{equation}

The Gram-Schmidt method depends on an order of the input and yields slightly different results when rearranged.
Some thought should be put into this choice to ensure correct interpretation of the results.
The first dimension should clearly be the valence direction. It is the one most decisively identified by participants. It is
also the most commonly agreed dimension in literature with some research using it as the only measured variable.
Table \ref{tab:ortho_data} shows the results starting with valence and then ordered by decreasing 1-norm of the remaining vector.

\subsection{Orthogonalized results}

The orthogonalization started with the empirical measurement for valence $(v^e)$. Being the first vector it was
not changed by the process.
Figure \ref{fig:emp_valence} shows the result as a graphical transformation, which
can be interpreted to sway the perceived sentiment from negative to positive most efficiently, i.e. with
minimal graphical displacements.
For the visualisation it is applied to a central face, which was chosen at the parameters $(0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.25)$.
This is a purely arbitrary choice. It just so happens to be in the center of the domain with a bias to
the non-contemptuous symmetric expression. Any other parameters could have been chosen as the center, because the
emotional effect of the graphical transformations were measured across the entire domain and not at a single point,
or along a single axis. From the chosen center all parameters are moved in proportion to vector $v^e$ until they reach the boundaries of the drawable space in the unit cube.

\subsection{Transformation}

Formula (\ref{eqn:transform}) summarizes the transformation from empirical coordinates into Cartesian ones for plotting.
They are ordered by decreasing effect on the reported emotional impression.
Each entry changes the impact of its emotional component without affecting the previous ones.
The function maps the default point $(0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.25)$ onto itself.

\begin{equation}
  \left(
    \begin{array}{c}
      v \\ a_1 \\ a_2 \\ d \\ c
    \end{array}
    \right) =
    \left(
      \begin{array}{ccccc}
        +1.00 & +0.32 & -0.25 & -0.24 & +0.22 \\
        +0.13 & +0.18 & +0.44 & +1.00 & +0.48 \\
        -0.11 & -0.13 & -1.00 & +0.56 & +0.07 \\
        -0.39 & +1.00 & -0.14 & -0.07 & -0.04 \\
        -0.30 & -0.10 & -0.09 & -0.47 & +1.00 \\
      \end{array}
    \right) \left(
    \begin{array}{c}
      v^e \\ d^e \\ k^e \\ a^e \\ c^e
    \end{array}
    \right) + \left(
    \begin{array}{c}
      +0.03 \\ -0.50 \\ +0.83 \\ +0.31 \\ +0.48
    \end{array}
    \right)
    \label{eqn:transform}
\end{equation}

The entries in the matrix are scaled to map each axis through the default point into a valid line within the unit cube.
This ensures that each individual axis can always be visualised with the appended plotting function.
Off-axis points in the empirical domain may be mapped outside the unit cube of the Cartesian model.
This is not such a big problem. As long as $a_1$ and $a_2$ are positive and keep the eyes at least as open as shut,
the face can be extrapolated and
recognized with some acceptance for artistic abstractions.

\subsection{Algorithmic emotions}

Before concluding this article I want to spend a few thoughts on a possible reinterpretation of the five dimensions.
All existing descriptions of emotions tend to anthropomorphize the entity that experiences them.
It is unclear if emotions will ever be experienced by anything other than us humans or our close relatives.
The first application of the presented visualisation technique could be the detection, aggregation and display of human emotions.
However, there is an interesting case where these data are genuinely generated by some algorithm.
Regardless of whether technical systems can really feel or just show these emotions, it doesn’t really matter, they are still theirs.
We will have to talk about them, even before we fail to distinguish their natures.
Therefore I want to provide a possible interpretation of the emotional dimensions such that they may apply to biological and technical systems alike.

\section{Conclusion}

This article introduced a visualization technique for a numerical embedding of emotional experiences.
The generated graphical icons translate a five dimensional input space linearly into line positions and angles.
Hence, the visualized parameters are extractable by looking at geometric properties. Simultaneously, the
graphical icons align well with how corresponding experiences are commonly depicted in comics and art,
showing an extremely exaggerated and stylized version of real faces in such situations.
The visualization combines the advantages of a linear gauge chart with unstructured emoticons.

The visualization method is named Paramoji in analogy to the widespread emoji, with the difference
that representable icons are not enumerated in a discrete sequence. Instead, the input parameters
define the icon's appearance in a space of meaning on a continuous scale. An empirical study among
international crowd workers revealed that most of the emotional contents could be related to
textual labels intuitively and without explanations. The two dimensions of pleasure and dominance
were recognized almost without distortions. Control and arousal were not understood as
orthogonal, but the independent component aligned with the model. The emotional label of contempt was not
recognized with significant fidelity, but its unique asymmetrical expression is well established in research.

\section*{Code}

The source code of the Paramoji may look compressed, but it is actually very easy to check for linearity.
A sparse matrix contains all values that define the transformation from input to drawing parameters.
There is a row for each free parameter in the drawing instruction and up to 6 columns
for each entry in the transformed input vector $(1, 2v-1, a_1, a_2, 2d-1, c)$.
The dot product is computed between each matrix row and the input vector,
which yields a value for each question mark in the template string.
The actual template might be difficult to deconstruct, but for the
scope of this discussion it is sufficient to understand that it is an SVG format
with a well documented limited feature set confined to numeric coordinates and affine transformations \cite{svg12}.
Hence, the linearity of the transformation can be confirmed without going into further detail.
Both, the JavaScript code and the SVG instructions, can be interpreted by any contemporary
web browser.\footnote{A demo of this code is currently deployed at \url{https://paramoji.org}}
The code guarantees valid faces for the parameters between 0 and 1, but off-domain values
can sometimes give recognizable results shown in figure \ref{fig:extra}.
