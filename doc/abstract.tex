A key challenge in human-computer interaction is the understanding and processing of
emotional experiences in terms of measurable data. Emotional states are often
expressed in categories of described subjective feelings, such as anger, fear and happiness.
This approach is impractical for
computational purposes, as there is no agreed-upon total number of emotions, let alone a metric for their
similarity, distance, or combinability. As a result,
computational processes typically rely on dimensional models using
independent scales like valence, arousal, and dominance. While these systems allow
for precise declarations of emotional states, there remains a lack of consensus on
how such numerical representations would actually feel.

This paper presents a method for mapping a numerical emotion model to a graphical representation,
resulting in facial expressions that evoke the corresponding emotions when viewed. Empirical results
show that untrained crowd workers could intuitively interpret the visual representation in alignment
with the numerical model. This indicates that the visualization effectively conveys emotional
information unique to each input. The drawing instructions rely solely on concise linear transformations,
minimizing interpretational ambiguity.
