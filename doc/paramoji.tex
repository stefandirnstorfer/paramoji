\begin{abstract}

 Efforts in the field of affective computing rely on some method to represent a human emotion in digital form.
 Two main approaches have emerged to accomplish this task. The traditional approach describes an emotion along
 a set of independent dimensions, such as valence, arousal, and dominance. This is mathematically elegant, but suffers from
 lacking consensus on how to represent more complex emotions. More recent approaches use discrete emotional states
 that are indicated by facial expressions or well described basic emotions.
 These have been popularized through the widespread use
 of emojis that humans can instantly recognize and interpret.
 However, due to their categorical nature they lack support for the computation of mid points, averages and other
 statistical measures.

 This paper seeks to bridge the gap between these two approaches by suggesting an emoji style visualization of an
 emotional model with five dimensions.
 These parametric emojis (paramojis) turn common emotional dimensions into facial expressions that
 can easily be interpreted by humans.
 They can parameterize most face emojis, as well as the seven universal emotions described by Paul Ekman.
 To test their effectiveness I hired untrained crowd workers to annotate a large set of photos
 from three different datasets used by the affective machine learning
 community. The obtained parameters are then compared with the results reported in the original publications.
 The suggested approach is essentially a visualization technique; its usefulness should
 be judged on the clarity of presentation. No evidence for missing emotional expressivity could be found.

\section{Introduction}

The field of affective computing was introduced by \cite{Picard97} with the goal to make computers
recognize, express, and in some cases, ``have'' emotions.
Its major challenge lies in the question of how to represent a human emotion in computer software.
Current approaches are based on statistical methods that measure various attributes of an
emotion or label them according to discrete categories.
This is happening while almost the entire internet has moved to the use
of early emoticons and now emojis for that same purpose.
It might seem natural that affective computing applications make use of emojis as a primary language
for the exchange of information about emotional states. However, this is not happening.
There is currently no known application
where a computer interacts with a human through the meaningful help of emojis.
What makes emojis so intuitive for humans but yet so hard to process for computers?

A large body of emojis have been standardized by the Unicode Consortium for the use on computer networks.
They started as depictions of facial expressions and sometimes objects that
describe situations, feelings and emotional states. Although other purposes
have been added it is that emotional quality that made them successful.
Despite intensive research the question of their objective meaning is still difficult to answer. \cite{emoji_review}.
There is even a significant mismatch in the way that humans interpret
some of the emojis.
While the standardization may define an emoji as eg. a ``grinning face'' \cite{emoji_interpretation} counted
at least 17 different versions of how this face may be displayed on various software platforms. The consequence is
a severe misunderstanding on even basic attributes of sentiment from positive to negative.
Another approach to get a more objective interpretation studies the anatomy of the facial expressions in emojis.
\cite{emoji_facs} have used an adapted version of the facial action coding system
to infer muscle activity and the causal emotions.
A coarse group of 11 emotional units was extracted, but exact interpretation varied widely based
on how they were rendered on various platforms and software versions.
The bottom line is that standardized Unicode emoji are
not suitable to encode nuanced and precisely defined emotions.

Outside the body of Unicode many emoji sets have been graphically designed to capture fine grained scales of emotions.
Research has found them useful for the communication of qualias, such as pain and taste.
\cite{likertscale} tested two smiley face Likert scales. These are sequences of emoji that encode a gradual increase of approval.
Two effective emoji scales were designed to capture levels of perceived pain within children and adults. \cite{bakerwong, dvprs}.
It was found that visual emoji scales produced more stable and consistent results in self-reported pain and greatly improved pain treatment.
A wider range of emotional experiences can be expressed if additional dimensions are allowed.
The circumplex model of affect is a widely used 2-dimensional model by \cite{circumplex} that can be visualized as facial expressions.
\cite{emojigrid} designed such an emoji grid and found it useful for reporting food elicited emotions.
Despite the additional depth of two dimensions the grid still lacks space for many emotions.
Additionally, the grid was only defined for emojis on the boundary of the domain without an obvious way to interpolate their visual appearance.
In short, these custom emoji sets are nuanced on very limited domains. They fall greatly behind in terms of expressable range.

For a greater range of facial expressions there is a visualization technique that can directly generate comic style
faces from high dimensional data.
Chernoff faces provide unique facial expressions for 18 parameters covering the
sizes of eyes, mouth and ears as well as angles and distances between them. \cite{chernoff}.
Unfortunately, this does not include any of the subtle activations that are necessary for the display of deep emotions.
The overall graphical quality is also limited by the technical constraints of the early 1970s.
Nevertheless, I think that the Chernoff face comes closest at being a predecessor, or next best solution, to the one I present in this paper.
Both approaches share two central features. First, they both use the human face to visualize high dimensional data.
Second, neither has yet produced a success story in any practical application.
In the following sections I will do my best to make that change.

\section{The parameters of the parametric emoji}
The idea of expressing a human emotion along measurable dimensions is said to date back to \cite{wundt}, who derived
his ideas mainly through self-inspection.
In a more accessible summary of his work his three dimensions of feeling were translated as pleasantness, excitement and tension by \cite{tridimensional}.
The first two of these dimensions have become the universal backbone of emotion classification, now known as "valence" and "arousal".
A much more intense debate formed around the third dimension. This paper follows the nomenclature of "dominance" by \cite{dominance_mehrabian}.
The fourth dimension is ``contempt'', derived from a discovery that this feeling of superiority is not a distinct emotion, but combines differently with positive and negative feelings. \cite{contempt_ekman}.
As a final dimension I will use ``control', which follows a simplified model for conscious modulation.
Hence, the interpretation of the last dimensions may partly be subject to cultural norms.

It is easy to understand all these dimensions in isolation, but in combination it becomes quickly unclear how to interpret them.
To solve this problem I want to present a visualization in the form of a parametric emoji (paramoji) that provides a
unique and valid facial expression for each combination of parameters.
The face is made to be generic, without any reference to physiological properties, like age, gender, color.
It is the strength of the emotional expression that had the top priority.
With a small exception in contempt, all graphical parameters depended linearly on the emotional dimensions.
Linearity ensures that all paramojis can be smoothly interpolated and animated into each other without jumps or unintended easing.
Although animation is not the topic of this document it is obviously the next frontier of affective computing, because the temporal development of
facial expressions unveils deeper insights that must be parameterized and visualized too.
For all the compromises that had to be made the result should be viewed as a graphical contribution rather than
a psychologial one.

\subsection{Valence and arousal}

\cite{circumplex} developed the circumplex model of affect as a quantitative framework
to describe all emotional states with two numerical parameters: ``valence'' and ``arousal'.
Valence describes the emotion along the axis of pleasure vs misery and approval vs disapproval. Arousal describes the axis
from aroused to sleepy, active to inactive or agitated to calm.
The claim that all emotions fall into a plane of only two dimensions might appear as quite a stretch.
However, this simplicity makes the model quite attractive and it became the basis of almost every emotional model.
Recent neurological evidence provides further assurance that these dimensions actually exist and correlate to
measurable brain activities. \cite{circumplex2}.

Figure \ref{fig:circumplex} shows the chosen visualization for the two dimensions.
The graphics is defined through the control points of Bezier lines with some rotation of the eyes' outlines.
All coordinates and angles are mapped linearly from the two input dimensions.
In this (still incomplete) model arousal controls eye and mouth sizes and valence controls curvature.

There is an obvious zero point for arousal when eyes and mouth are totally shut.
On the other end of the spectrum there is no physical limit on how wide a comic eye can open.
Therefore, arousal values can only be compared against each other without an objective center.
For valence the natural center is a flat mouth and flat eyes.
Eyes and mouth bend upwards for pleasure.
This is consistent with a respective muscle movements and was already part of early emoticons as \texttt{\^\_\^} and \texttt{:)}.
Eyes bend downwards for misery. This is derived from the foreshortened view when the head is lowered.
A combination of pleasure with a lowered head, ie a smile with eyes bent downwards, is prevented by the linearity constraint.
\footnote{A Unicode emoji for such an expression exists with code 1f60c.}

Looking at facial expressions one can find the predicted emotions on the edges of the domain:
alarmed \paramojib{50,100,100,50,0},
sleepy \paramojib{50,0,0,50,0},
happy \paramojib{100,50,50,50,0} and
miserable \paramojib{0,50,50,50,0}.
The quadrants also comply with the circumplex model, showing
contentment \paramojib{100,0,0,50,0},
excitement \paramojib{100,100,100,50,0},
distress \paramojib{0,100,100,50,0}
and depression\paramojib{0,0,0,50,0}.
The original publication postulates many more emotions that are supposed to sit tightly packed on a planar space.
However, fear vs anger vs tension, bordom vs sadness: these are fundamentally different emotions that can hardly be seen in close proximity to each other.
More dimensions are needed to separate them.

\subsection{The third dimension: Dominance}

A first candidate for a working three-dimensional model was the evaluation-potency-activity model \cite{potency_osgood}.
It was developed to find the main axes of meaning in nouns by mapping them to adjectives through a rigorous data driven approach.
Factor analysis revealed that two of the dimensions with most explanatory power were equivalent to valence and arousal.
The third of the three most important dimensions of meaning is described by the adjective scales of strong vs weak,
hard vs soft, brave vs cowardly, tenacious vs yielding.
Unfortunately, the author did not find a good name for it.
The pleasure-arousal-dominance model by \cite{dominance_mehrabian} fixed that problem. Its dimensions are
introduced as ``analogues'', because the results of the factor analysis replicated the dimensions with slightly changed names.
In this model anger, power and boldness dominate over anxiety, infatuation, fear and loneliness.

Irrespective of naming and definition details, the third dimension - dominance - shows anger and strength \paramojib{50,50,50,100,0}
on one end of the scale against fear and weakness \paramojib{50,50,50,0,0} on the other end.
The visual effects are lowered inner eyebrows, inwards rotated eyes, more prominent display of teeth and a sharper mouth contour.
For low dominance the visual effects are exactly mirrored, because all displacements are, again, linearly added.
Figure \ref{fig:dominance} shows four of the previous paramojis, which were assumed to have a neutral dominance of 0.5 in the center.
Left and right the paramojis show lowest (0.0) and highest (1.0) values of dominance.
Obviously, the scale is continuous and all intermediate values could be interpolated.

The three parameters, valence, arousal and dominance, range from 0 to 1 and span the unit cube domain.
Four corners on the low valence side correspond to misery and disapproval. They already resemble some
preliminary versions of the basic emotions:
fear \paramojib{0,100,100,0,0},
anger \paramojib{0,100,100,100,0},
sadness \paramojib{0,0,0,0,0} and

For positive valence we find pleasure and approval. Two universal emotions sit on the positive end of the cube:
happiness \paramojib{100,50,50,50,0} and
surprise \paramojib{100,100,100,50,0}.
The expressions in the corners of the positive side are probably not universal. I'll call them
simper \paramojib{100,0,0,0,0},
embarrassed \paramojib{100,100,100,0,0},
anticipating \paramojib{100,100,100,100,0}
and a forced smile \paramojib{100,0,0,100,0}.


\subsection{Contempt}

The final missing universal emotion is contempt \paramojib{50,50,0,50,100}.
It is described by the adjectives
scornful, haughty, smug, vain, or disdainful. \cite{contempt_ekman}.
Comtempt is a feeling of moral superiority that varies in expression based on the amount of social support
one gets. Contempt against inferiors, the feeling of power can be a pleasurable experience.
Comtempt against a superior can feel misarable. Because the expression depends on other dimensions it is a dimension
itself, rather than a specific spot in parameters space.

The only universally confirmed expression of contempt is the ``unilateral lip-tightening expression''.
A raised upper lip often shows in conjunction disgust. Tension in the cheek can also press the eyes into
squeezing. A squinting eye is not in itself a signal for contempt,
but a graphical choice to emphasizes its asymmetric nature and,
it often occurs in coincidence.
Figure \ref{fig:contempt} shows random emotions with maximized contempt levels. At least graphically,
contempt mixes perfectly with the previous dimensions. The left eye and the left side of the mouth have a bilinear
graphical dependence. Respective coordinates depend on $c$, but also on pairwise products with the other dimensions.
All combinations of parameters produce comical, but valid facial expressions.

\subsection{Control}

The final dimension of this model describes the modulation of a facial expression that is partly put under conscious control.
It ranges from controlled, suppressed \paramojib{50,100,0,50,0}
to uncontrolled, expressed \paramojib{50,0,100,50,0}.
The visualization follows an hypothesis raised by \cite{Ekman75}. Their studies suggested that ``when a person is
controlling what is shown on his face, more effort is focused on managing what occurs in and around his mouth and
lips than in the area of the eyes/lids or brows/forehead.''  Although there might be exceptions to that rule,
it is certainly good enough as a first approximation to the subject matter.

The major and easiest to control aspect of an emotion is its intensity. In visual terms, this is arousal ($a$). All
the graphical impact caused by $a$ can be separated into arousal shown in the eye region ($a_1$) and arousal
shown in the mouth region ($a_2$). The amount of excerted control ($o$) can be computed as the difference between
these two. For a consciously deintensified expression $o$ is large. For an intensified expression $o$ is low. The dimensions
$a_1$ and $a_2$ are mathemtically preferred, because they are defined on a cartesian domain. The dimensions $a$ and $o$
are closer to a possible interpretation, but do not produce valid visualizations for all combinations of values.

Equation (\ref{eqn:control}) defines how these two dimension pairs can be converted into each other.
The complexity of these mathematical equations is commensurate with the psychological depth of the model.
In the end its the visualization that counts, not the numbers.

\begin{equation}
 \label{eqn:control}
 \begin{array}{rcl}
  a_1 &=& a + o/2 \\
  a_2 &=& a - o/2 \\
  o &=& a_1 - a_2\
 \end{array}
\end{equation}

Figure \ref{fig:control} shows the visual effect of the control dimension on some random random faces.
It is interesting that even in the extremely uncontrolled versions one can still differentiate between
positive \paramojib{100,0,100,50,0}
and negative \paramojib{0,0,100,50,0} valence.
This is in contrast to real faces, where extreme pain and extreme joy cannot be distinguished from
the facial expression without context information or body language \cite{painjoy}.
Emoji style visualization are not just exaggerated versions of existing facial expressions.
They can also encode emotional information that go beyond what real faces show.

\section{Data collection}

In the previous section I described my subjective choices for the paramoji's visual appearance.
In this section I will lay out my process to obtain objective values for its parameters.
In particular I wanted to know what parameters random participants would choose for some photos with facial expressions.
The results are checked for consistency with existing annotations from the respective data sets.

\subsection{Workers}

The question at hand is, if the paramoji can be intuitively understood, without any training or instructions.
To test the hypothesis test participants were hired from MicroWorkers.com, a global market place for crowdsourced work.
A deep analysis of the workers' anatomy has been made by \cite{microworkers} and found to be still valid.
The overall work force has a global bias towards India, Bangladesh and the Philippines with some participation
from the Western countries.
No relevent effect could be linked to country or language.

Crowd workers were offered 20 Cents for the classification of a batch of 20 facial expressions.
Hence, the cost for one low quality classification was 1 Cent. On the fastest path this takes two clicks to complete.
The median time was 10 seconds per image; the mean was 20 seconds. A very small amount of workers
took minutes to complete and frequently revised their selection before submitting it. No correlation
was found between speed an any
conceivable metrics for quality.

It is very likely that many workers have seen multiple versions of the paramoji questionnaire during a period of
about 8 years
in which the design was subsequently improved as an occasional hobby project.
Never did they get any instructions or feedback on the quality of their classifications. All workers
were paid without additional quality control. No submissions were excluded from the final analysis.
Workers were only allowed to participate once per experiment, ie. contribute 20 parameterization, to avoid confounding
influence from preexisting qualifications.
Only a truly neglegible number of workers submitted destructive results by e.g. always clicking on the same position.
Overall work participation was very good and consistent, albeit noisy. Depending on accuracy requirements 10 to 100
classifications per image should be considered.

\subsection{User interface}

Figure \ref{fig:encoder2} shows a possible user interface that uses sliders to select the values for each individual
dimension.\footnote{A version of this interface is currently deployed on https://paramoji.org/editor.html}
This input method is very user friendly and intuitive. However, it creates biased results.
The values obtained from sliders have are clustered at the center position, with some spikes
at the extreme values. I also suspect - without evidence - that users are attracted to certain pattern that
are formed by multiple slider positions stacked upon each other. Sliders are extremely powerful for fine tuning,
but unexperienced users are prone to abuse their regularity for mental short cuts.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
 \begin{center}
  \includegraphics[height=5cm]{gen/encode2.pdf}
  \vspace*{-3ex}
 \end{center}
 \caption{
  A natural user interface with sliders for each dimension. This allows for highly accurate
  input, but the results are biased by order, orientation and naming. Hence, this interface is not suitable
  for quick annotations by untrained participants.
 }
 \label{fig:encoder2}
\end{figure}

Figure \ref{fig:encoder} shows a low bias user interface for paramoji selection. It does not allow
users to select or see the dimensions individually. They can only choose the best fit from a small
number of randomly parameterized options. Because every possible selection is completely random no
bias is created if users prefer to click at certain positions or press randomly to speed up their work.
An accurate result can easily be obtained through repeated measurement and statistical aggregation of
the numerical parameters.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
 \begin{center}
  \includegraphics[height=5cm]{gen/encode.pdf}
  \vspace*{-3ex}
 \end{center}
 \caption{
  Low bias, low accuracy user interface.
  Initially, users see 15 random paramojis sampled from a prior distribution.
  Upon first observation (ie. user clicks) the prior is updated. Users had to repeat this process with
  20 different images. No additional explanation or instruction was given. This input method is
  very inaccurate, because users can only select from few totally random options. Due
  to low bias, the experiment can be repeated and the medians of the underlying
  parameters converge to a reproducible result.
 }
 \label{fig:encoder}
\end{figure}

Equation (\ref{eqn:prior}) shows the prior distribution from which the initial options are sampled.
Each user starts with their own random sample from $x_1,\cdots, x_{15} \sim P(v,a_1,a_2,d,c)$.
After the first click on a paramoji $x_s$ the prior is updated to focus in the area of the selection. The new
options are shown as $\tilde{x}_i = (x_i + x_s)/2$. Another option is to restart the process with a new random sample.
Before the final selection users saw at least 29 different options.
80\% of users sticked with the first selection. 20\% refined their selection at least once when more choices were presented.

\begin{equation}
 \label{eqn:prior}
 \begin{array}{rcl}
  P(v),P(a_1),P(a_1),P(d) & \sim & [0,1] \\
  P(c) & \sim &
  \left\{
  \begin{array}{ll}
   [0,1] & \mbox{with 50\% probability} \\
   0 & \mbox{otherwise}
  \end{array}
  \right.
 \end{array}
\end{equation}

The marginal distribution over all selected samples corresponds
largely to the prior distribution. Any randomness created by low motivation or missing understanding would not
create any bias other than a posterior sample distribution that is closer to the prior.

\subsection{Data selection}

In this final section on data collection and bias control I want to address the issue of selective inference.
None of the numerical results are on a level that would be worth faking, but - that said - they could be worse.
Most of the analyses that preceeded the results below have been done on an unannotated photo sets and benchmarked against
standard emojis for inter-annotator agreement. The published results are almost a first shot in benchmarking
against pre-annotated data from the internet. Unfortunately, the first run was done with an older version of paramoji, where
contempt raised an upper lip proportional to $c$, leading to a contemptuous face to always show traces of disgust.
Hence, paramojis were not able to reproduce the rather friendly contempt faces from the AffectNet data set.
After fixing the problem, all analyses were rerun without significant changes other than the parameters for the
contempt class of AffectNet data.

\section{Results}

In this section I want to apply the introduced method to the data sets of three different affective computing projects that deal with
the digitzation of emotions or facial expressions. All humans have agreed to use emojis for this purpose.
It is not entirely clear, why current affective computing applications don't. Whatever the reason may be,
paramojis can easily translate between numeric methods and emoji-like visualizations.

\subsection{EMOTIC data set}

The EMOTIC data set is a collection of photos with humans shown in a social context compiled by \cite{emotic_2019}.
Many photos show a group of people interacting or individuals during an engaging activity.
For every person in the photo an annotation with respect to valence, arousal and dominance is provided.
The purpose of the data set is to train a machine to recognize the emotions that are elicited by the context,
rather than by the face alone. Many photos do not show faces at all, either they are hidden by objects like helmets, or
they are shown from the back.

Table \ref{tab:emotic_samples} shows the three examples as highlighted in the original publication.
Next to the images you can see the parameters obtained by the original and by my method,
which is the median annotation of 100 crowd workers. The median is generally preferrable over the mean, because
it is less biased against the boundary.
Since the resulting values differ you are free to agree with either approach. I dare to argue
that it is easier to have an opinion about mine. The emotional impact created by the available
visualization is hard to escape.

\subsection{AffectNet data set}

The AffectNet data set is a collection of annotated images of human faces by \cite{affectnet}.
It is unarguably the largest set of annotated photos showing human
facial expressions.
It includes annotations for valence, arousal and categorizes all faces into
8 classes: Normal, Happy, Sad, Surprise, Fear, Disgust, Anger, Contempt.\footnote{The AffectNet data set is not freely available to non-academics.
Therefore a sampler from Kaggle.com had to be used. Unfortunately, it was missing valence and arousal annotations.}
The difficulty of assigning faces to only one of those classes has been described by \cite{affectnet_relabel}.
It was found that little agreement was reached within a small group of annotators for many images with multiple cues.
Annotation qualities were confirmed for valence values, but for arousal the agreement was only reported as ``medium''.
AffectNet data is widely used as a benchmark for facial expression recognition
applications and is the perfect basis to calibrate the paramoji model to a widely accepted standard for 8 base expressions.

Table \ref{tab:affnet} shows the median rating for randomly selected images from the
AffectNet data set.
The parameters in this experiment have not been obtained by annotating single images with high accuracy.
These are median values of ratings spread over a class rather than focused on single images.
The result shows that the average emotion in each class conforms with the expectation.
It also reveals some artefacts in the way AffectNet is clustered.
A high level of contempt in the disgust group shows that these two emotions often occur together. Since disgust seems to mask out
contempt as the dominating emotion the remaining images in the contempt class are unduely happy.

The ability to do meaningful statistics over a large set of noisy emotion parameters is not self evident.
If averages were taken over pixel values or facial key point coordinates this result could not be obtained.
It would just produce a gray image or an emotionless standard face. This is a confirmation that the
chosen emotional dimensions actually work as intended. The visualized paramojis are
scaled four times against the overall mean to get the exaggerated version of the facial expression.
This scaling also corrects the diluting effect of AffectNet label as well as crowd worker errors.
This works surprisingly well and offers a method for the robust aggregation of low quality emotion measurements
with possibly much wider applications.

The paramojis in figure \ref{tab:affnet} are the result of empirical measurements. Hence, they
are not necessarily the best choice to represent the class from a theoretical perspective.
The following is a quick check of the correctness of the facial features against
their original definition provided by \cite{Ekman72}:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item {\bf Happiness:}
  Eyes relaxed and narrowed; lower lid pushed up; outer corners of lips raised; opening mouth and teeth
  appearing; lips are pushed back. The only missing feature are the crow feet at the out edge of the eyes.
 \item {\bf Sadness:}
  Brows drawn together with inner corners raised and outer corners lowered; bulge or muscular
  contraction above center of brow area; drooping upper lid; lax lower lid; lid pulled up at inner corner,
  down at outer corner; eyes look downwards; outer lips pulled down. Check.
 \item {\bf Surprise:}
  Raised curved eye brows; wide open eyes showing sklera above and below the iris; skin above eyelid streched,
  dropped open mouth; lips are parted.
  The major missing feature are the relaxed outer lips. The crowdworkers chose parameters
  towards high valence, which raises eye brows and outer lips.
 \item {\bf Fear:}
  Raised, drawn together eye brows; short horizontal eye brow wrinkles; eyes opened,
  hard stare. The mouth is opened, outer lips are drawn back.
  There should be more sklera on top of the pupil and none below. This is missing, but would be possible
  with less arousal as seen in figure \ref{fig:dominance}, top, left.
  Another mismatch is the outer edges of the lips drawn down, instead of sideways.
  There is a bias towards low valence.
 \item {\bf Disgust:}
  Brows drawn down; vertical creases in forhead and mouth; lower eyelid pushed up, but not tense;
  deep nasolabial fold; upper lip raised; lower lip pushed ``outwards'', slightly opening the mouth.
  The biggest miss is the brows drawn together too much. Also missing is the wrinkle on the bridge of the nose.
  The strong appearance of contempt is also not necessary.
 \item {\bf Anger:}
  Brows pulled down; strong vertical eye brow wrinkles; upper lids appear lowered; lips pressed together
  or showing of teeth.
  The major issue here is too much skelra in below the iris.
 \item {\bf Contempt:}
  The unilateral lip-tightening. \cite{contempt_ekman}. Should be less happy though.
\end{enumerate}

The classification into only 7 non-normal categories is clearly inspired by the universal emotions propagated by Paul Ekman.
However, it misses the point in many aspects. An exclusive classification into either-or categories is totally against
the spirit of the Ekman's results. Disgust and Contempt, for example, are highly correlated and often occur together.
Other categories, like surprise, are also explicitely described to mix well with other emotions. Although Ekman focuses on
7 categories of emotions, he never claimed that this is a comprehensive list of all experiences with universal facial expressions.
Pain, for instance, has an objective stimulus and distict facial expressions as measured by \cite{pain}.
It should be summarized that this type of categorical classification is not a good basis to describe a detected
emotion.

\subsection{FEC data set}

The FEC data set is collection of image triplets for which human annotators marked the odd one out.
\cite{fec}.
These data can be used to train and test algorithms for facial expression similarity.
The judgement on which of the faces look more similar was only made on the basis of facial expression, without
consideration of color, gender, hair style or make up.
A paramoji encodes facial expressions with 5 parameters and explicitely limits itself to dimensions
with emotional interpretation. It is obvious that the FEC model with 16 parameters and no such limitation performs better.
Nevertheless, it is interesting to explore by how much.

Figure \ref{fig:fec_samples} shows the three example triplets taken from the original publication.
The triplet in each row corresponds to one of three cases, depending on how many different
emotional classes the images were assigned to in the AffectNet data set. Theoretically, the second class
should be the easiest, because one of the three images shows a distinctly different emotion.
However, real emotions can blend into each other and expressions on the border of two neighboring classes can
look more similar than two images from the same class.

The numerical results in table \ref{tab:fec_results} show the recognition rate after converting them into paramoji.
This was achieved by 600 workers annotating 479 images from 236 triplets with an average number of 25 annotations per image.
The parameterization was considered successful if the the Euclidean distance between two parameter vectors was minimal
in accordance with the voting majority of the original annotators. When annotators could only see one image at a time
and all knowledge about the facial expression had to encoded into a paramoji the success rate was 60\%.
This is the same experiment that was described in the original publication, with the only difference that
annotators did see not the triplet directly. A median worker with direct access to all three photos achieved 88\%.

In addition to the human performance the FEC team also published two results from machines: their own model, which
they promoted, and a neural network trained on AffectNet data, which they referred to as ``next best solution''.
Since AffectNet has only 8 emotional categories it is not immediately obvious why it would score well on such a
task at all. The reason is that neural networks are typically very good at building a more elaborate
model of the problem domain in some of their higher layers, before condensing the result to a single label in a
final voting layer. It is interesting that paramojis perform on the same level as the penultimate layer of the AffectNet
trained network (AFFNet-Cl-P) with some ``human'' margin. This would indicate that whatever knowledge the
neural network builds does not exceed
the scope of what could be expressed with paramojis. It could be an indication that the emotional
model behind them is fairly complete with respect to known emotional revelations from a single facial expression.

\section{Conclusion}
A lot of effort has been spent on decoding the emotional content from facial expressions.
The suggested approach goes the opposite direction. It creates a facial expression from
emotional dimensions. Unlike ubiquitous emojis the suggested paramojis do not visualize
distinct emotional categories. Instead, they create an emoji style visual representation
from five continuously scalable parameters.

Provided empirical evidence supports the hypothesis that untrained people could intuitively
decode the emotional content of a paramoji. Without any further instructions randomly hired
crowd workers were able to associate faces from the seven basic emotions defined
by Ekman and Friesen with distinctly separate paramoji parameter groups. The visualizations
of those parameters yielded facial expressions that showed most, but not all of the predicted features
in a strongly exaggerated manner. The resulting graphical representations are clear and impactful.

It was also shown that the emotional model behind paramojis might be a good training target for
machine learning algorithms. This would have a number of benefits. First, a unified framework
for continuous and categorical dimensions would avoid annotation incongruencies. Second, the
exaggerated visualization makes it much easier for untrained participants to consistently and
reproducibly annotate large amounts of images at low cost. Third, additional information about
the structure within categories as well the consistent representation of border cases would
offer additional applications, such as statistical post-processing.
Fourth, the ability to visualize results would make it much easier to check an annotation's
correctness. Fifth, affective computing would be much more fun if smileys replaced boring numbers.

Finally, it is time for an outlook on possible future steps.
Will it ever be possible to fully capture human emotions with computer software?
Probably not, but let's try anyway. It is known that temporal developments reveal much more
insights into the inner feelings than a static expression. \cite{Ekman75} provided
a rich framework to analyse the timing of shown emotions in the context of conscious emotion
modulation. Popular social media networks are already moving from emojis to small GIF animations
that show a sequence of facial expressions in animated loops.
I firmly believe that this is the next frontier for affective computing.

\section*{Code}

The source code of the paramoji might seem confusing at first. However, it is extremely simple, once broken down.
A sparse matrix contains all the parameters. It has a row for each parameter in the drawing instruction and a column
for each entry in the vector $(1, 2v-1, a_1, a_2, 2d-1, c, ca_1,cv, ca_2, cd)$.  The product of the two is inserted
for the question marks in the template. The output format is in SVG, a standardized description language for
vector graphics. Both, the JavaScript code and the SVG instructions, can be interpreted by any contemporary
web browser.

The easiest way to run the code at home is as follows: 1) Open the developer console in your web browser.
2) paste the source code into the console and press enter. 3) Type: \texttt{document.write(paramoji(1,1,0,0,0))}
and press enter again. The facial expression should appear on your screen.
